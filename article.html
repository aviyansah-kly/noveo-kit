What founders should expect from a 12–24 month forecast.

A useful forecast is not a prediction. It is a structured way to see the financial effect of decisions before the business commits cash.

By NOVEO AdvisoryUpdated September 2026Forecasting

Most founder forecasts fail for one of two reasons: they are either too simple to support real decisions or too complicated to stay current. The right answer is not more spreadsheet detail. It is a model that connects the operating assumptions leadership actually controls to the financial outcomes leadership actually cares about.

Start with the decisions, not the template.

Before building a model, ask what decisions it needs to support. A SaaS company may need to compare hiring pace against retention and acquisition efficiency. An agency may care more about utilization, pricing, delivery capacity, and client concentration. An e-commerce business may need inventory timing, paid acquisition, gross margin, and working capital in the same view.

The forecast should be designed around the business model and the decisions ahead—not around a generic finance template.

Separate assumptions from outcomes.

A forecast becomes useful when leadership can see which assumptions are driving the result. Revenue growth, churn, headcount, pricing, gross margin, collection timing, and acquisition spend should be visible enough to challenge and update.

That separation matters because when reality changes, the team can adjust the assumption instead of rebuilding the model from scratch.

Three assumption groups are usually enough to start.

  • Commercial: pipeline, conversion, pricing, retention, acquisition, and revenue timing.
  • Operating: hiring, capacity, delivery cost, vendors, inventory, and major planned spend.
  • Cash: collection timing, payment timing, financing, taxes, and one-off commitments.

Use scenarios instead of one “official” future.

A single forecast can create false confidence. Scenario planning is more useful because it shows how the business behaves when a few important assumptions change.

A practical model usually includes a base case, a conservative case, and one decision-specific scenario such as accelerated hiring or higher acquisition spend. The goal is not to create dozens of versions. It is to make the tradeoff visible.

Make the forecast part of the operating rhythm.

The best model is the one leadership keeps using. That means actual performance should be compared with plan regularly, major assumptions should be updated when the operating picture changes, and the forecast should show what those updates mean for cash, margin, and runway.

If the forecast only appears before a board meeting or fundraising process, it is not yet part of the finance operating system.

What should leadership be able to answer?

A useful 12–24 month forecast should make questions like these easier:

  • How long does cash last under realistic—not optimistic—assumptions?
  • What happens if we hire these roles three months earlier or later?
  • Which revenue or margin assumptions create the biggest downside risk?
  • How much capital would we need under each growth path?
  • When does additional growth spend stop improving the business?
  • Which metric should trigger a change in plan?

The forecast is a conversation tool.

The real value is not the spreadsheet. It is the quality of the conversation around the assumptions, tradeoffs, and next decisions. When the model is clear enough for leadership to challenge, finance becomes more useful before the commitment instead of more accurate after it.
